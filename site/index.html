      01

      AI football commentary that shows its working

      watches a football match and commentates on it, like a broadcaster would. The difference: before any line goes on air, a second AI checks every fact in it against what actually happened on the pitch. If a claim can't be proven, it doesn't air.

      What it does

      As a match plays, one AI spots the moments that matter: a goal, a big chance, a team taking control. Another writes the line a commentator would say. A third, the Critic, checks the line against the match's raw events. It recounts the passes, re-measures the distances, checks that every event cited had actually happened yet. Only then does the line reach the screen, with its evidence attached and a receipt showing what it cost.

      Why I built it

      AI writes fluent sentences, and fluent isn't the same as true. In sport, a wrong number on air costs the broadcaster the viewer's trust. I wanted commentary you can audit: every claim traceable to the moment it describes, and a system honest enough to say "I can't prove that" instead of making it up. If a model fails mid-match, the broadcast keeps going and says so.

      02

      AI can talk about football. It can't prove what it says.

      Language models write fluent commentary in seconds. They also invent numbers, mix up minutes and cite moments that haven't happened yet. On a live broadcast, a wrong line reaches every viewer before anyone can check it.

      Confident and unchecked

      A model sounds just as sure when it's wrong. Without a check between the draft and the screen, every error goes out live.

      One feed for every fan

      An analyst wants expected goals and pressing patterns. A casual viewer wants to know why the crowd just roared. Most feeds serve one and lose the other.

      English first, everyone else later

      The game is watched around the world, but live commentary is mostly written in English. Urdu and Arabic speakers usually get a translation, not commentary written for them.

      When the model stalls, the feed goes dark

      A live system that waits on one slow or failed model call goes quiet at exactly the moment the match needs it.

      04

      How a claim gets on air

      Every event takes the hot path: fast, deterministic, no language model. Each alert then takes the warm path, where one agent writes, another checks, and nothing airs until it passes.

      One claim, followed all the way

      The claim from the front page, through each step of synthetic match seed 7.

      1. Spot

        The Scout reads every event as it arrives and flags goals, big chances, great passes, breaks from a ball won in a side's own half, a high press paying off, swings in momentum, and fouls with a card or a penalty in them.

      2. Write

        The Narrator drafts the line for air from a sheet of facts code computed, and cites the event behind every one. On Azure, a model in Microsoft Foundry writes it: gpt-4.1-mini, chosen by evaluation.

      3. Check

        The Critic recomputes each number from the raw events, with the same formulas the Scout uses. A wrong number, a missing citation or an event from the future sends the claim back, up to three attempts.

      4. Air

        The Localizer turns the verified claim into an on-screen cue for casual fans or analysts, in English, Urdu or Arabic, with its player tags and a receipt of what was checked and what it cost. A claim that never passes never airs.

      5. Make it yours

        A viewer picks a club, a player, or one number to follow. Their moments come first and carry their club's colour; the other side's still air, marked as the rival's. Following one player keeps only that player's moments, counts the rest, and adds a live card counted from the player's own events. The models tell the line from the viewer's side. The facts don't move: every line still passed the Critic.

        56'GOAL · SAL 2–2 BRK

        Your clubYour player

        Bilal Arholm scores for Saltmarsh Rovers from 10 m, a speculative chance (xG 0.11). Set up by Adem Rosmere's 22 m pass, difficulty 58 of 100.

        Synthetic match seed 21, with Saltmarsh Rovers as the viewer's club and Adem Rosmere as their player.

      6. Recap

        At full time the match writes its own recap: the result, every goal, the numbers for both sides, the moments that aired and the players who made them. Each line it writes goes to the Critic first, with one rule lifted: it may look back over the whole match. A whole-match number must be counted to the final whistle, and the line cites it.

        SAL 2–2 BRK, the numbers
        Shots: SAL 19, BRK 10. Expected goals: SAL 2.82, BRK 1.64.

        Checked by the Critic, citing all 29 shots and the final whistle.

      7. Ask the match

        A viewer can ask the match anything, live or after full time. The Narrator answers, but it learns a number only by calling the Scout's tools: code that counts the events so far. Each count comes back as a fact with an id, the answer names the facts it uses, and code builds the claim from them. The Critic then checks it like any line: every count recomputed, every number and every player it names backed by those facts. What the events can't answer, like an injury or the final score, gets plain fixed words, not a guess. With the voice on, ask out loud in English, Urdu or Arabic: Azure AI Speech turns the question into words, and the checked answer is read back in the same language.

      Plug it into a broadcast

      Everything the court produces is built for someone else's system to use, not only this studio.

      • A cue stream with a contract

        Each line leaves as a timed, prioritised JSON cue with its evidence, its receipt, the players it tags and, for a foul, the card to draw. A JSON Schema, written from the code and checked on every push, is the contract a graphics system builds against.

      • The Critic, for any assistant or agent

        Six read-only MCP tools, so an assistant can read a match and put a claim to the same Critic before it says anything. And an A2A agent with a card, so another agent can hand it a claim or a question as a peer. Same rules either way.

      • An audit trail

        Every cue, refusal, answer and change of service level, kept for 90 days on Azure Cosmos DB: what aired, who wrote it, what was refused and why.

      • The cost of a line, known

        A receipt on every cue: the model, its tokens and its cost. A model-written line costs about $0.0007, a whole match under a cent.

      • The recap, as a page

        One standalone page per match that prints to PDF, every line it wrote checked by the Critic.

      • Your rules, your languages

        The competition picks how strictly fouls are read. English, Urdu and Arabic, on screen and spoken aloud.

      05

      When an agent fails, the broadcast keeps going

      The Orchestrator supervises the line. If the Narrator or Critic stops responding, it retries once. If the agent is still down, viewers get detection-only cues, marked as unverified, and the broadcast says when full service is back.

      1. Full serviceEvery cue verified by the Critic

        The match ledger

        A blockchain's integrity, without a blockchain. Every event, line, refusal and answer of a match is hashed and sealed, minute by minute, into blocks that each name the one before. Change one byte anywhere and it shows.

          Check a real match, in your browser

          The ledger of the match recorded from the live studio: 1,815 events and every line, refusal and answer. Your browser recomputes every hash itself, with nothing taken on trust.

          Download the ledger

            What makes it hold

            • Every entry hashed. The match itself, every event, every line that aired, every claim the Critic refused, every answer, and the recap. SHA-256, over one canonical form.
            • A block a minute. Each minute's entries are sealed by a Merkle root, built the way Certificate Transparency builds its logs (RFC 6962).
            • Each block names the one before. Change an entry and its block's root changes, so its hash, so every block after it.
            • One seal. The last block's hash stands for the whole match. Copies are kept apart from the ledger, in the audit trail on Azure Cosmos DB and in the trace.
            • Anyone can check. In a browser, or offline with python -m court verify, which also generates the synthetic match again from its seed: the ledger must hold exactly those events.
            • One answer, a few hundred bytes. Each answer's proof is its Merkle path and the blocks on to the seal (RFC 9162). The Ask page checks it in the browser.
            • No coin, no mining, no betting. Nothing to buy, nothing to trade. It's there to keep the record honest.

            New to hash chains and Merkle roots? Watch them explained in eight animated scenes — including what happens when you change one entry on purpose.

            What it proves, and what it doesn't

            It proves the history hasn't changed since it was sealed, and that the events are the ones the seed generates. It adds nothing a line didn't already prove, and a studio that lied while recording would seal its lie: the copies of the seal kept elsewhere make that harder, not impossible. Publishing each match's seal to a public log is the next step.

            What this data can't tell you

            A system that checks its claims should say where its checks stop. These are the limits as they stand today, measured where they can be.

            • It doesn't predict, and it's not for betting

              only tells you what has already happened, from the match events. It never predicts a result, a score or the next goal, never shows odds, and won't help anyone bet: ask it about bets or odds, in English, Urdu or Arabic, and it says so in fixed words before any model reads the question. Enjoy the football, not the odds. If betting is causing harm to you or someone close to you, free and confidential support is there to help.

            • Momentum calls are sure, not complete

              On matches never used for tuning, 62% of the Scout's momentum calls match a real swing, and it catches about a quarter of the swings there are. It's tuned to say less and be right, so when it stays quiet a swing may still be under way.

            • The football is synthetic

              Every club, player and match is invented by code. The generator matches real volumes over 100 seeds (about three goals, 29 shots, 78.5% of passes completed and 320 pressures a match), but no real team plays in it, so nothing here says anything about real clubs or players.

            • Events, not video

              The court sees what the event stream records: passes, shots, tackles, pressures and changes of possession, each with a place and a time. It doesn't see runs off the ball, a team's shape between events, or anything only a camera would. Speed and distance tags come from the events' own fields: a shot's speed, a pass's length.

            • Its own xG

              Expected goals here come from a small, open formula (distance, angle, headers, pressure), the same one the Critic recomputes with. It's consistent, not a trained provider's model: its numbers aren't comparable with a broadcaster's.

            • True isn't the whole story

              The Critic proves that a line's numbers and citations hold. It doesn't judge emphasis or fairness, so a verified line can still be one-sided or dull. Checked means supported by the events it cites, and nothing more.

            • Translations are checked for numbers and names

              An Urdu or Arabic line may state no number the English line doesn't, and must keep every name. The wording itself is the model's, reviewed by a person, not by the Critic.

            • Standouts are counted, not rated

              The recap's players who made it happen are counted from their own events. There's no rating behind them and no per-player rule for the Critic yet, so the recap marks those lines as counted, not checked.

            • The ref's call is a reading, not a referee

              It sees where a foul happened and what it stopped, never the force or the speed of the challenge. It states a likely decision at a stated strictness. The restart has ground truth (the generator awards penalties the same way); the card has none yet.

            • The voice reads names as best it can

              Names stay in Latin letters in every language, so the Critic can check them; an Urdu or Arabic voice may say an invented name oddly. The words it speaks are exactly the line that aired.

            • An answer's judgment words are the model's

              When you ask the match, every number, name and citation in the answer is checked. Words like "dominated" or "the better side" are the model's reading of those checked counts, not a verdict the Critic can prove. Ask for the numbers and judge for yourself.

            • Making it yours changes emphasis only

              A favourite club or player changes what comes first and what's marked as yours. The facts are the same for every viewer.

            Trust is the product

            A model can already write commentary. The open question is whether anyone can rely on it, live, with millions watching. is built around that question.

            “We will see whether you have told the truth.”

            King Solomon to the hoopoe, Surah An-Naml 27:27. Why that line is the name

            1. Nothing unverified goes to air

              Broadcasters answer for every word on screen. Each claim carries its own audit trail: the events behind it, the check it passed and the attempt it took.

            2. The model writes, the evidence decides

              I don't ask the model to be careful. I check its work with the same formulas the Scout uses, and refuse anything that doesn't add up.

            3. Commentary in the fan's own language

            A Microsoft Reactor session presented by Brian Benz, October 14, 2026. Three ideas from it are on the build log: a receipt on every answer, letting evaluation pick the model, and saying plainly what the data can't tell you.
            eFootball performance report
            A report by BETER, September 2026, on esports football during a major international tournament, written for betting operators. Three ideas from it are in HUD² and the roadmap: big tournaments bring newcomers, so the fan's lens explains in plain words; the experience of a match matters as much as the match; and interest runs before, between and after matches. Because it is about betting, it also settled a principle: never predicts, never shows odds, and discourages gambling. Its figures are the supplier's own: I took ideas from it, not evidence.
            Certificate Transparency (RFC 6962 and RFC 9162)
            The IETF's design for logs no one can quietly rewrite. The match ledger builds its Merkle trees and checks its proofs exactly as these specify.

            Most of these buttons work only for me

            The site and the media kit are public. The studio and the Critic's MCP endpoint are private, behind Cloudflare Access: only my email address can sign in, and a machine needs a token only I hold. The Azure and Cloudflare buttons open dashboards in my own accounts. To follow them you'd have to be signed in as a member, with access granted to you. Anyone else gets a sign-in page or a "no access" message. They're on this page to make my own work easier, not for visitors to browse.

            The Azure links contain my subscription's and resources' identifiers, which grant nothing without access; the Cloudflare links leave the account out. Nothing in the project holds a key: every Azure service signs in with Microsoft Entra ID, and the studio checks Cloudflare's signature on each visit instead of a password. For everyone else, the proof is further down this page.
